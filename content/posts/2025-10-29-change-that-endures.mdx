# Change That Endures: Lessons from the Hard Road of Improvement

> 💡 **Core Insight**  
> Real improvement doesn't happen when you convince everyone — it happens when you build habits that outlive you.

---

## The Mirage of Change

When I first worked as an internal change agent at Chrysler, I had the privilege of helping drive real progress.

We modernized how teams worked, delivered meaningful outcomes, and built momentum that people could feel. Those experiences shaped how I think about improvement to this day.

But as proud as I was of what we accomplished, I couldn't shake the feeling that it wasn't *enough.* The improvements we made were powerful — but local. They didn't always spread across the enterprise or stand the test of time when priorities shifted.

The first time I went to the DevOps Enterprise Summit in 2018, I was captivated. Every speaker seemed to have cracked the code to lasting change.
But after returning for several years, I began to notice a pattern:

> Most success stories were about small pockets of excellence —
> heroic efforts that lasted until the hero left.

That's when it hit me: enduring improvement isn't about isolated wins.
It's about rewiring how an organization learns and adapts.

---

## The Work Continues

That realization still guides me today in my work at Planview (formerly Tasktop).
I've had the privilege of learning from some of the best — Dominica DeGrandis, Carmen DeArdo, and Mik Kersten among them — and I've worked with dozens of organizations trying to improve how they deliver value.

Across all of them, the same patterns show up: bursts of enthusiasm, pilot programs that fade, metrics that lose momentum.

<img src="/achieve.jpeg" alt="Achievement" class="magazine-image" />

But one engagement — a very large financial services company on the U.S. east coast — showed me what happens when improvement actually sticks.

---

## The Company That Got Better — and Stayed That Way

This company wasn't new to improvement. They'd lived through Agile, Lean, and DevOps — and learned something from each. They'd gotten good at operationalizing change.

From the beginning, they treated **Flow** not as another initiative, but as a *capability to build.*

We anchored on a clear north star:
reduce **flow time** and **work in progress (WIP)** across the enterprise.

They tied these goals to **OKRs** — measurable outcomes that aligned teams and leadership.
Suddenly, every team had a reason to care.

We formed a central **enablement team** of about ten people whose mission was to teach Flow thinking, coach teams, and embed it into daily ceremonies.
For six months they prepared, and for a year they ran hard.

At first, progress was slow. But then the data started to show real improvement.
Across roughly **800 teams** and nearly **10,000 people**, flow time began to drop and WIP stabilized.

---

## When Change Took on a Life of Its Own

Going into year two, leadership decided to scale back the central team.
I worried that would be the kiss of death.

But it wasn't.

The habits had taken root. The OKRs, the momentum, the daily Flow conversations — they'd become part of how people worked.

<img src="/machine.jpeg" alt="Leadership and Direction" class="magazine-image-left" />

Even after the enablement team disbanded, **flow time continued to drop — more than 50% across roughly 800 teams, covering nearly 10,000 people.**

But another lesson emerged from that experience — one I didn't expect.

---

## The Missing Ingredient: Experimentation

Early in the journey, I had encouraged leadership to track their improvement experiments.

> "If you're going to make improvements,", I told them,
> "track what you're changing and why. Otherwise, you'll never know what caused the result."

I proposed a simple metric — a certain number of experiments per quarter — but leadership decided against it. They felt the teams already had too much on their plates.

So we pressed forward without it.

By the end of the first year, as we began seeing signs of improvement, leadership didn't feel much had changed.
They asked managers, *"What did you do differently?"*
Most said, *"Nothing really."*

Yet the numbers kept improving.



I ran correlation analyses — first between Flow metrics and Viz usage (not compelling), then between Flow improvement and **deployment frequency**.
That's when the light bulb went on.
The correlation was strong and unmistakable.

It took time for the realization to settle in, but eventually leadership saw that the improvement was real — and lasting.

**Lesson learned:**
Change is hard to perceive while it's happening.
Even when progress is real, it doesn't *feel* like progress.
That's why you have to track your experiments as you go — not after the fact.
Correlation later is helpful, but learning in real time is powerful.

---

## What Makes Improvement Endure

Enduring change isn't a campaign.
It's when the *organization itself* starts sustaining momentum — when progress stops being driven by heroes and starts being driven by habits.

But here's another hard truth:
You have to **establish standards and direction** — even when it's uncomfortable.

Every big improvement effort invites a crowd of opinions.
People step out of the shadows with their preferences, their exceptions, their "what abouts."
It's human nature.

The trap is trying to please everyone.
I've seen so many efforts stall because leaders spent months debating complaints instead of picking a direction.



If you want to get good at change, you have to get good at deciding.

Sometimes you just have to bite the bullet, set the standard, and move forward.
That's what separates organizations that *talk* about agility from those that *practice* it.

At Chrysler, I saw passion, ingenuity, and teams that truly cared.
At this financial institution, I saw what happens when that same spirit of improvement is scaled — with structure, alignment, and data guiding the way.

---

## Closing Reflection: From Flow to What's Next

From my experience, I've had the good fortune to see improvement from many perspectives — as an internal champion, an external consultant, and across multiple levels of leadership.

Seeing it from all sides has given me a deep appreciation for the challenge — and what it actually takes to reach *real* improvement.

Flow time improvement — delivering software faster and more predictably — is part of the journey. But it's never been the *goal.* The real goal has always been **happy customers** — customers who feel the impact of faster feedback, better quality, and products that meet their needs when they actually need them.

For the first time in my career, I've seen that happen. I've seen teams not just deliver faster, but deliver *better* — and felt the ripple of that change reach all the way to the customer.

Ironically, the world has already moved on to the next big thing. **AI is the new frontier** — promising to reshape how we learn, decide, and deliver.

<img src="/flow.jpeg" alt="Flow Network" class="magazine-image" />

But what I've learned through Flow still applies:
AI won't succeed because of tools alone. It will succeed when we teach it context, discipline, and purpose — the same ingredients that make improvement last.

Someone once told me, *"Your success isn't defined by whether others notice it."*

They were right.

I know what happened.
I was there.
I saw it.

And that's enough — for now.
Because the next challenge is already here.
